Which AML alerts get escalated?

We had 14,000 labelled alerts and about 7 million historical transactions, and our task was to rank 6,000 unseen alerts by their probability of escalation. This page explains how we explored the data, what we found, and how those findings shaped the model.

Our approach

  1. Understand the data layout. Each alert (signal_id) links to many transactions. We checked the time window covered by each alert's history, how the train and test sets are split, and what the standardised size index represents.
  2. Look for separating behaviour. We compared escalated and dismissed alerts on transaction volume, mix, size, timing and direction. We measured each candidate signal by its univariate ROC-AUC, so that weak effects didn't get buried.
  3. Aggregate the history into features. We built 277 features for each alert, including distribution statistics for each direction × type combination, recency windows, flow balances, drift, and a pooled "size level".
  4. Validate honestly. Train and test cover the same dates, so we used 5-fold stratified CV repeated over 3 seeds. We kept only changes that beat the fold-to-fold noise (about ±0.003 AUC) and ran ablations on each feature group.
  5. Ensemble and calibrate. We blend the ranks of a shallow LightGBM, a small CatBoost and a logistic regression, then apply Platt scaling to the blend. Scaling doesn't change the ranking, so AUC is unaffected, but the outputs become real escalation probabilities.

Dataset overview

The data has two levels: one row per alert, plus a long table of the transactions that preceded it. Alerts span two full years, and the train and test alerts are spread evenly across the same months. This is a random split, not a time split, which is why we used stratified K-fold instead of time-based validation.

Target distribution

About 1 in 6 alerts is escalated (). The classes are imbalanced but not extremely so. ROC-AUC ignores the base rate, so we didn't resample, and we calibrated the final probabilities to the observed rate. The escalation rate stays flat over the calendar. It rises gently with the number of transactions: busier accounts get escalated slightly more often.

Transaction behaviour

Cards and bank transfers make up about 93% of all transactions. Incoming transactions (kirim) outnumber outgoing ones (chiqim) roughly 3 to 1. The size index is a continuous, log-like score: each type has its own level, and international transfers sit about 2 standard deviations above card payments. Every type has a hard upper cap (for example, cards stop at 4.18), and there are no repeated or round amounts.

The mix barely separates the classes. Escalated and dismissed alerts have almost the same share of each transaction type (cards 52.2% vs 52.1%, cash 6.5% vs 6.2%, escalated vs dismissed). Whatever drives escalation, it isn't simply "more cash" or "more international".

Activity over time and right before the alert

We normalised each alert's history to a total of 1, then averaged the daily activity profile separately for escalated and dismissed alerts. The two profiles are almost identical: regular activity peaks 4–5 months before the alert and fades steadily over the final ~3 months. The one sharp structure is at the very end: of all transactions happen in the final 10 minutes before midnight of the alert date, a median of transactions per alert, spread evenly over the last ~3 minutes.

Escalated vs dismissed: where the signal is

The strongest single pattern is in bank transfer size. Escalated alerts have relatively more small bank transfers and fewer large ones. This is a smooth, monotone shift, not a threshold effect, so we saw no structuring spikes just below a limit. Grouping alerts by their average bank-transfer size, the escalation rate falls from about 22% in the lowest decile to 12% in the highest.

Weak signals that add up. No single feature exceeds 0.575 AUC. The signal is diffuse: many small, mostly monotone effects spread across the direction × type size distributions. That's why a linear model on rank-transformed features gets within 0.008 AUC of gradient boosting, and why strong regularisation (depth 3, 7 leaves) beat deeper trees.

Experiments we tried and dropped

  • Transaction-level model (each transaction inherits its alert's label, predictions pooled per alert): 0.54 AUC. Too noisy, dropped.
  • Deeper / longer CatBoost (1,500 iterations, depth 6): 0.623, clearly overfitting. The small version (400 iterations, depth 4) reaches 0.646.
  • Burst-only features: AUC 0.51–0.52 on their own; kept, but they add little.

Other checks

  • Alert ID order: escalation rate is flat across ID deciles (15.9–18.5%). No leakage; not used.
  • Alert date / month / weekday: flat. Kept as weak features, but ablation shows no gain.
  • Round or repeated amounts: only 145 of 7M values repeat, and those are just the per-type caps. No structuring signature.
  • Share of cash / international: nearly identical between classes.

Conclusion

  • The data is a random split with a 180-day history per alert and a 17.2% escalation rate. Stratified CV matches the test setting.
  • Escalation is driven by the size profile, not the mix. Escalated alerts are associated with smaller (especially outgoing) bank transfers, a different card-size range, and slightly higher activity.
  • The signal is weak and diffuse. The best single feature scores 0.575 AUC; combining about 270 history aggregates lifts this to about 0.65 OOF.
  • Simple and regularised models win. Shallow LightGBM, small CatBoost and a logistic regression, rank-blended and calibrated: OOF ROC-AUC.
  • Timing is mostly an artefact. Hour, weekday and the pre-alert burst carry almost no information.

The data is synthetic and contains no real customer records. The code is fully reproducible from the team notebook (notebook.ipynb): feature engineering → CV → ensemble → submission.
